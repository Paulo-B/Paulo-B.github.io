There are a lot of games out there: games that make you feel happy, others sad, others that make you wonder why the fuck you are even playing that. Others are only entertaining, others you forget in less than a month, and others stay in your mind for a long time. But independent of that, the reason they stay in your mind is because they did something that connected with you, and because they were what they intended to be and showed you that. There is a phrase about writing critiques of any piece of art that has stayed in my mind since the first time I read it. You should judge art for what it is trying to be, not for what you think it should be. With that in mind, I will judge it without knowing the intentions of the developers. For games, at least, that seems the better way to critique them, because you, the reader, will not know them either. You do not care that the developers are some dudes from Australia called Beethoven & Dinosaur, or that the publisher is Annapurna Interactive, or that the director is Johnny Galvatron. Or do you?

Anyway, like the title of this review says, I consider this not to be a game with a soundtrack; I consider it a soundtrack with gameplay. The game follows the story of the teenagers Slater, Cassandra, and the principal protagonist Stacey Rockford, on their last day together. They are looking to have a good last day party before Stacey goes to New York City to follow her dream of being a music supervisor (a weird name for a music curator). The game, through various elements in the scenario, tells you more about the characters: their stories, how they met, what teenage problems they had, what they have and have not done together, their personalities. In this aspect, the game does the work it should do. The characters are what you expect: teenagers who want to be teenagers, a glorified version of teenagers like you would see in the movies. In some cases they can be close to nothing in terms of relatable, but in others, in the dialogue and the reimagination of the scenes with some minigames, they express, in a light-hearted way, the various emotions of being that age: sadness, being apart from your friends, feeling free, and so on. But it is only that; it can be a little flat in the interpretation, or cringe sometimes (but being a teenager is cringe). Do not expect it to be the most well thought out or philosophical, nor the greatest plot.

That said, the gameplay? There is some, but only in the form of interactions with objects, so you learn more about the people and the stories (one of the ways of progressing). The others are some kind of minigames (yes, the cringe one with the tongues too) and things like running in a field. None of them are hard, and almost none of them have a way to fail. They are there only to make you interact with the game, and only that. Do not expect anything more from that.

But, to me, with the perspective I gave you, the reader, at the beginning, this is not a game; it is a soundtrack, a Mixtape. And that is where the game excels. The song choices, although I do not have the knowledge to know if they are well known for those times or are more niche selections, are perfectly paired with the action, with the feelings, with the story, so much so that it makes the game feel unnecessary and you just want to feel the emotions and the story only by listening to the songs. And let them fill your emotions in each part. I can say that some songs, like Yesterday's Hero, Roads, Freak, and That's Good, will be on my headset sometimes, even if they are nothing like the songs that I usually pick for myself. And together with that, there is the thing I have not talked about: the stop-motion-like graphics that give gorgeous views and scenes.

The game is short (3 to 4 hours for $19.99), but full of good vibes. Do not think much about it, just feel it, and leave your analytical mind for another time.
